\begin{abstract}
Sorghum (\textit{Sorghum bicolor}) is a climate-resilient crop whose acclimation to nutrient limitation and low temperature likely involves extensive lipidome reconfiguration. Lipids are key membrane components, carbon and energy stores, and mediators of stress signaling, yet population-scale lipidomics data for sorghum are limited. We present the Sorghum Lipid Database (SoLD), a curated lipidomics resource from the Sorghum Association Panel grown under two field regimes, (i) a nutrient-sufficient with usual planting date environment (control) and (ii) a low-input treatment with reduced nitrogen and phosphorus, earlier planting, and no application of insecticides, herbicides, or pesticides (low-input). Using high-resolution LC--MS, we quantified 243 lipid species and detected broad, largely conserved compositional shifts across field trials. However, there were four major low-input-associated lipid signatures relative to control, (i) depletion of sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol, (ii) triacylglycerol enrichment, (iii) phospholipid redistribution centered on phosphatidylserine, and (iv) coordinated lysophospholipid remodeling, reflected in altered lysophosphatidylcholine-to-lysophosphatidylethanolamine ratios. Analyses of lipid chemical space and lipid ontology enrichment supported these compositional changes. GWAS of lipid species, class sums, and class ratios revealed recurrent, environment-specific loci. Control-associated loci were enriched for genes involved in lipid and isoprenoid metabolism, developmental regulation, and cell-wall biosynthesis and modification. Low-input-associated loci were enriched for genes involved in nutrient-stress signaling, cell-wall remodeling, defense, developmental control, and cold-related barrier formation and proteostasis. Thus, SoLD provides a framework connecting sorghum lipid diversity with environmental and genetic variation. All information regarding the database and the experiment is freely accessible through a Shiny application: \url{https://nirwan.shinyapps.io/SAP-Lipidomics-Database/}. The database enables users to move from lipid-class to individual molecular species and associated candidate loci, for hypothesis generation, comparative analyses, and prioritization of targets for functional validation.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Plants routinely face environmental stresses that limit yield, such as nutrient limitation and low temperature. Nutrients such as nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) deficiencies are important for research because they restrict overall growth, nutrient allocation, and developmental plasticity, while low temperature disrupts cell membrane fluidity, metabolism, and yield \citep{Schluter2012, Silveira2018, Emendack2021, Ding2019, Guo2025}. Lipids are central to these environmental responses as cell membrane components, carbon and energy stores, and precursors of signaling molecules. Stress-driven lipid remodeling can alter membrane stability, photosynthetic capacity, storage-lipid abundance, and lipid-derived signaling \citep{Bohn2007, Siebers2015, Hou2016, Rawat2021, Li2020, Bhattacharya2022, Xu2025}. Under P limitation, plants usually replace phospholipids with non-P galactolipids and sulfolipids, including digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG) and sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG). Cold stress can shift acyl-chain composition, phospholipid turnover, and triacylglycerol (TG) accumulation \citep{Essigmann1998, Hartel2000, Jouhet2004, KellyDormann2002, Tan2018, Pfaff2020, Yan2025, Zhang2020}. These lipid responses are followed by nutrient and stress-regulatory pathways. An example includes the SPX-domain proteins, which regulate phosphate signaling through PHR-centered networks \citep{Lv2014SPX4}. Similarly, nitrate transporters such as NRT2 and NPF mediate N acquisition and allocation, and sulfate transporters influence plastid sulfur supply for sulfolipid metabolism \citep{Lupini2016NAR2, Sorgona2011Nitrate, Xia2015NPF24, Chen2019SULTR3}. In conjunction, lipid-remodeling enzymes, including non-specific phospholipases, DGATs, and SDP1-like lipases, mediate phospholipid turnover, DG production, and TG synthesis and mobilization \citep{Bose2021NPC, Ngo2019NPC, Tan2018, Kelly2011SDP1L, Yen2008DGAT}. However, population-scale lipidomics resources linking lipid remodeling, nutrient-stress, and natural genetic variation remain scarce in crops, particularly in sorghum.

Sorghum is a stress-resilient cereal crop with large genetic diversity. The Sorghum Association Panel (SAP) is a collection of sorghum lines with substantial genomic variation that captures broad botanical, geographic, and genetic diversity \citep{Casa2008CommunityResourcesSorghum, Boatwright2022}. This makes it ideal for studying natural variation due to environmental stresses. For lipidomics, however, population-scale interpretation requires careful data processing and annotation. LC–MS lipidomics can be affected by run-order drift, instrumental batch effects, and field-layout artifacts, while lipid annotation and naming can vary across studies. Here, we have used a QC-based normalization method, SERRF; spatial correction methods, SpATS; and a spectral annotation database, GNPS, that improve reproducibility and biological interpretation \citep{Fan2019, RodriguezAlvarez2018, Wang2016GNPS, Molenaar2019}.

A second challenge in population-scale lipidomics linked to GWAS is interpreting large candidate-gene sets. GWAS across many lipid species, class sums, and class ratios can yield hundreds to thousands of nearby genes, but LD-assigned genes are not independent. A single association can tag multiple neighboring genes, including tandem arrays with similar annotations, so standard gene-level enrichment can misinterpret one locus as pathway-level evidence. This issue led to GWAS-specific gene-set and interval-enrichment tools such as INRICH, SNP2GO, and Gowinda, which adjust for LD, candidate-SNP structure, or gene-level biases \citep{Lee2012INRICH,Szkiba2014SNP2GO,Kofler2012Gowinda}. Here, we extend this approach by testing Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment against an LD-aware interval-permutation null and by distinguishing terms supported by multiple independent genomic intervals from those driven by single annotated loci.

Here, we present the Sorghum Lipid Database (SoLD), a curated lipidomics and GWAS resource for the SAP. Leaf samples collected between the V6 and V8 stages were profiled under two field regimes: a control environment (CTL), grown in 2019 with sufficient nutrients and standard planting, and a low-input regime (LIN), grown in 2022 with reduced N and P, earlier planting to impose early-season cold-associated stress, and minimal chemical management, including no insecticide, herbicide, or pesticide application. Since CTL and LIN were grown in different field years, we interpret their contrasts conservatively as between-trial differences rather than direct causal treatment effects. 

We combine LC--MS lipidomics, compositional analysis, lipid ontology, GWAS of individual species and class-level traits, LD-aware GO enrichment, LINEX-based reaction mapping, and an interactive Shiny application. This framework identifies four major LIN-associated lipid signatures: SQDG depletion, TG enrichment, PS-centered phospholipid redistribution, and altered LPC/LPE balance. It also prioritizes candidate functions linked to nutrient acquisition, DG--TG cycling, phospholipid turnover, sphingolipid metabolism, and stress-associated membrane remodeling, including representative loci such as DGAT1 (\texttt{SORBI\_3010G170000}) for TG accumulation, a SULTR3-family sulfate transporter (\texttt{SORBI\_3006G244000}) for SQDG-related sulfur supply, and an SPX-domain phosphate-status (\texttt{SORBI\_3001G028500}) candidate. Together, SoLD provides a resource for exploring sorghum lipid variation, candidate loci, and accessions for future validation. The interactive SoLD Shiny application enables exploration of all datasets and results and is available at \url{https://nirwan.shinyapps.io/SAP-Lipidomics-Database/}.

\section{Results}

\subsection*{QC and normalization yielded comparable LC-MS performance across conditions}

We profiled early-stage lipids from SAP grown in two field conditions defined earlier. The workflow (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s1_workflow}) comprised tissue collection and extraction, high-resolution LC-MS lipidomic profiling, GNPS-assisted lipid annotation, post-acquisition data correction with SERRF and SpATS \citep{Fan2019, RodriguezAlvarez2018}, and data dissemination via the SoLD Shiny app (\url{https://nirwan.shinyapps.io/SAP-Lipidomics-Database/}).

Run-order quality control showed stable analytical performance (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s2_qc}A--B), with the total ion current (TIC) and the summed signal intensity per sample meeting expected values across system checks, internal standards, and blanks. TIC variability among QC samples was low (7.5\% CV for CTL and 6.0\% CV for LIN). SERRF normalization improved technical precision, reducing median QC-RSD from 3.6\% to 2.0\% in CTL and from 1.6\% to 0.3\% in LIN. The proportion of features with QC-RSD below 30\% rose from 75.7\% to 94.1\% and from 77.3\% to 89.3\% respectively (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s2_qc}C--D).

PCA of normalized data showed tighter sample clustering than unprocessed data (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s2_qc}E--F). After spatial correction, the leftover variation in MGDG(18:3/18:3) is scattered randomly across the field rather than forming rows, columns or patches, indicating that position in the field no longer drives the signal (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s2_qc}G--H). Post-correction, lipid signal distributions remained similar across cohorts, and the adjusted trait matrix was used for all subsequent analyses.

\subsection*{Genotypic lipid variation is largely independent of botanical race and population structure except for PS}

The SAP is structured by botanical race and geographic origin \citep{Casa2008CommunityResourcesSorghum, Boatwright2022}. We next asked whether the lipid variation reflected that structure. We tested two groupings. The first was botanical race, collapsed into six groups: the five classical races (Bicolor, Caudatum, Durra, Guinea, Kafir) plus a Mixed group containing all intermediate designations (e.g., Durra--Bicolor, Kafir--Caudatum) (Supplementary Table~S25a). The second grouping was marker-based genetic cluster ($K=6$), which covered 387 CTL and 355 LIN accessions and provided a more complete genetic description of population structure. We tested 14 traits per condition, which included total lipid signal and the 13 lipid classes (Supplementary Table~S25) using Kruskal--Wallis tests with BH correction and summarized effect size ($\varepsilon^2$) described as the proportion of rank variance explained by the grouping.

Botanical race explained little to no lipid variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1_population_structure}A). After BH correction, no trait was significant in either trial (smallest $q=0.468$) and effects were small ($\varepsilon^2 \le 0.027$; Table~\ref{tab:race_structure}; Supplementary Table~S25). Marker-based genetic clusters likewise showed no association except for phosphatidylserine (PS) under LIN, where cluster membership was associated with PS abundance ($q=1.11\times10^{-6}$; $\varepsilon^2=0.104$; Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1_population_structure}B,D). Under CTL it showed only a weak, non-significant trend ($\varepsilon^2=0.020$; $q=0.358$; Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1_population_structure}C). A PCA of lipid class composition showed the same pattern. Accessions from the same race or genetic cluster were dispersed rather than clustered, each explaining at most 1.6\% of variation, with no significant tests (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s3_class_pca}).

Excluding whole ancestry groups produced results similar to explicit modeling. In each trial, we removed all accessions from one botanical race group and recomputed each lipid class’s share of the total annotated signal from the remaining accessions. Across six leave-one-group-out runs and 13 lipid classes, no class changed by more than 0.10 percentage points under CTL or 0.18 under LIN. The largest deviation in both trials was for phosphatidylcholine (PC; ~25\% of total signal). To test sensitivity to unstructured sampling, we generated 1,000 random 80\% subsamples and for every class, the 95\% interval stayed within 0.21 percentage points of the full-panel estimate (Supplementary Table~S5G). Overall, removing any single ancestry group or 20\% of the panel did not measurably alter trial-level lipid class composition.

Overall, botanical race and genome-wide genetic clusters explained little lipid-class variation, so the associations explained are unlikely to be artifacts of population stratification. The only clear structural effect was PS under LIN because PS is very low in relative abundance. This likely reflects redistribution within a minor phospholipid pool rather than a bulk lipidome shift.

\subsection*{Lipidome overview for CTL and LIN SAP}

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to visualize the lipidome structure, concentrating on major glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid pools (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s5_pca}). However, the other free fatty acids and minor lipid classes are available in the Shiny app. A total of 243 lipid species were quantified, 152 of them detected in both trials, 49 in CTL only and 42 in LIN only, whplied threshold in one trial rather than confirmed absence (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s4_counts}). TG and PC were the most diverse classes in each environment (TG: 71 in CTL, 69 in LIN; PC: 39 in CTL, 38 in LIN; Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s4_counts}). PCA showed that lipid class strongly structured lipid-species variation in both trials (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s5_pca}). TG species formed a distinct cluster, separate from most membrane lipids in CTL and LIN, though along different axes in the two trial-specific ordinations. Galactolipids and glycerophospholipids occupied partially overlapping but class-enriched regions, supporting class-level summaries for compositional and reaction-network analyses. Because PCA was run separately for each condition, the orientations of the principal components are descriptive rather than a direct CTL–LIN trajectory.

At the class level, we summarized lipid abundance as \%TIC for each retained annotated class, reflecting the relative signal rather than the absolute concentration. The lipidome was dominated by galactolipids and phospholipids. MGDG accounted for 32.3\% of the mean annotated signal under CTL and 29.8\% under LIN, while PC was similar in the two trials (24.0\% in CTL and 23.1\% in LIN). The remaining galactolipids and the diacylglycerols followed, with DGDG at 12.0\% and 12.1\% and DG at 12.9\% and 13.8\%. TG accounted for 2.6\% of the annotated signal under CTL and 5.0\% under LIN, and SQDG for 2.7\% under CTL and 1.3\% under LIN. The 13 focal classes together account for roughly 93\% of the annotated signal in each trial, the balance being sterols, terpenoids, prenols, fatty acids and the other minor categories (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2_class_composition}A,B; Supplementary Table~S5D). 

PS is the smallest of the focal classes in both trials, at 0.01\% and 0.04\% of the annotated signal, and it is the only class-level trait whose LIN values track genome-wide genetic clustering (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1_population_structure}D), so variation in this minor pool is genotype-dependent rather than uniform across the panel. Within each trial the class-level abundances are strongly coupled, and the correlation structure among classes is given for CTL and LIN separately in Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s6_pcor}. The lysophospholipids LPC and LPE are likewise minor, at 0.16\% and 0.01\% under CTL and 0.15\% and 0.01\% under LIN, consistent with stress-responsive turnover of a small pool rather than uniform scaling of lyso-lipid abundance \citep{Hou2016, Rawat2021}. Species counts by class and superclass are given in Supplementary Tables~S6a to~S6c.

Together, the abundance and compositional analyses nominate four features that characterise the LIN lipidome, (i) low SQDG, (ii) high TG, (iii) a PS-centered phospholipid balance, and (iv) a shifted LPC/LPE balance. We carry these forward as descriptive axes for the analyses that follow rather than as responses to a treatment. The two trials differed simultaneously in growing season (2019 and 2022), in planting date, and in nitrogen and phosphorus supply, and they were acquired in separate analytical batches, so these features describe the low-input trial as it was conducted and cannot be attributed to reduced nitrogen, to reduced phosphorus, or to earlier planting individually, nor can contributions from year, site and batch be excluded. The ontology, reaction-network, and GWAS analyses below further provide complementary biochemical and genetic context for these axes.

LION enrichment recovered the same shift and described it biophysically (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2_class_composition}C; Supplementary Table~S5E) \citep{Molenaar2019}. The strongest terms in either direction were a head-group charge contrast, with LIN depleted for \textit{headgroup with positive charge / zwitter-ion} and enriched for \textit{headgroup with neutral charge}. This reflects the concurrent decline in PC and PE and rise in TG rather than a uniform change in zwitterionic lipids, since the zwitterionic lysophospholipids LPC and LPE increased under LIN. Class terms mirrored this, with LIN enriched for glycerolipid, triacylglycerol, lipid-storage and lipid-droplet terms and depleted for glycerophospholipid and membrane-component terms. Membrane-property terms indicated thicker, less laterally mobile membranes under LIN, and acyl-chain terms were consistent with the species-level contrasts.

Furthermore, to characterize structural remodeling beyond total class abundance, we summarized each lipid class in a reduced chemical space defined by its abundance-weighted mean total carbon number and double-bond count across constituent molecular species, described once for each trial (Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s6_chem}; Supplementary Table~S5F). The classes separate cleanly on carbon number, with the lysophospholipids and monoacylglycerols near 17 carbons, the diacyl membrane classes clustered between 33 and 37, and TG far to the right at 50.9 carbons under CTL and 54.6 under LIN. Unsaturation orders the diacyl classes, from PG at 0.6 double bonds through PC at 2.1 to MGDG at 5.7. PE sits at 2.7 double bonds under CTL and 3.4 under LIN, the widest separation of any class on that axis.

Together, the abundance-, composition-, ontology- and chemical-space analyses describe the four axes above as a coordinated redistribution within the LIN lipidome rather than independent class-level shifts.

\subsection*{GWAS signal is concentrated under CTL and diffuse under LIN}

We ran separate GWAS for each individual lipid trait, lipid-class sum, and lipid-class ratio in both trials, using a univariate linear mixed model with the first three genotype PCs to account for population structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:gwas_manhattan}). SNPs surpassing the Bonferroni genome-wide significance threshold ($p \leq 8.19 \times 10^{-9}$) were mapped to candidate genes using the LD-based cutoff of 0.4. Under CTL this identified 1,100 candidate genes for individual lipids and 115 for class sums and ratios (Supplementary Tables~S7 and~S8). Under LIN the same procedure identified 4,323 and 812 candidate genes (Supplementary Tables~S9 and~S10). Manhattan and quantile--quantile plots for every lipid phenotype in both trials are available in the SoLD Shiny application.

Because LD assigns several genes to each association, we also counted the independent 250~kb intervals those genes occupy. The individual-lipid sets reduce to 378 intervals under CTL and 971 under LIN, so LIN recovers about 2.6 times more independent loci rather than the four-fold excess suggested by gene counts. The two trials also differ in how concentrated that signal is. Under CTL, 281 of 1,100 individual-lipid candidates were associated with seven or more phenotypes, and the most recurrent locus reached 27. Under LIN only 11 of 4,323 reached seven phenotypes and the maximum was 13. Because lipid phenotypes are strongly correlated by construction, particularly among TG species and the class sums and ratios derived from them, these recurrence counts are descriptive summaries of the candidate tables and not a test against a null expectation. The class-sum/ratio layer shows the same contrast, with a single CTL locus associated with 64 phenotypes against a LIN maximum of 20. CTL therefore yields fewer loci that each affect many lipid traits, whereas LIN yields more loci that each affect few.

In the CTL individual-lipid layer, the strongest association was \texttt{SORBI\_3001G522600} (CW-type domain-containing protein; $p = 4.50\times10^{-35}$; $r^2_{\max}=1.0$), and the most recurrent locus was \texttt{SORBI\_3006G040500} (phospholipid-transporting ATPase; 27 phenotypes; $p = 1.01\times10^{-26}$). In the CTL sum/ratio layer, the strongest association was \texttt{SORBI\_3010G136200} (SLH domain-containing protein; $p = 4.28\times10^{-23}$; $r^2_{\max}=0.91$), and the most recurrent locus was \texttt{SORBI\_3003G318650} (64 phenotypes; $p = 3.37\times10^{-12}$; $r^2_{\max}=0.90$).

In the LIN individual-lipid layer, the strongest association was observed for \texttt{SORBI\_3004G130500}, an unannotated candidate gene ($p = 2.44\times10^{-73}$; $r^2_{\max}=1.0$). The most recurrent individual-lipid locus was \texttt{SORBI\_3010G064200} (1,3-beta-glucan synthase; 13 phenotypes; $p = 3.40\times10^{-16}$; $r^2_{\max}=1.0$). In the LIN sum/ratio layer, the strongest association and the most recurrent locus were both \texttt{SORBI\_3004G004800} (soluble acid invertase; 20 phenotypes; $p = 2.61\times10^{-21}$; $r^2_{\max}=0.78$).

\subsection*{GO enrichment is specific under LIN and unspecific under CTL, and both rest on a small number of genomic intervals}

To test whether candidate genes converged on shared functions, we ran GO over-representation analysis separately within each lipid class, for genes associated with individual lipid species and for genes associated with class sums and class ratios, in each trial and in each of the two ontologies. Stratifying by lipid class is what makes a term interpretable, since it attributes an enrichment to the traits of one class rather than to the candidate set as a whole. Because a single association can tag several neighbouring genes, including tandem arrays, every term was also tested against an LD-aware permutation null built from gene-density-matched 250~kb intervals (Methods). Biological-process (GO-BP) and molecular-function (GO-MF) terms were tested and BH-corrected separately.

Of 103 enrichments, 101 passed the LD-aware null, so the permutation correction changed enrichment strength and ranking rather than membership. Two properties of the results then had to be handled before they could be read. First, the Gene Ontology nests parent and child terms, so one candidate gene set surfaces as several terms; second, class sums and ratios share candidate genes by construction, so one gene set recurs across lipid classes, trait layers and both ontologies. Merging enrichments with identical candidate-gene sets, first within a stratum and then across strata, reduced the 101 terms to 50 distinct loci, 17 under CTL and 33 under LIN (Supplementary Table~S16). The candidate genes carrying each term are listed in Supplementary Table~S19, and the uncollapsed per-term results in Supplementary Tables~S17 and~S18.

Two opposite failure modes show why neither statistical significance nor distributed support is sufficient on its own. First, a term can be specific but driven by a single locus. \textit{Purine nucleoside transmembrane transport}, \textit{purine nucleobase transport} and \textit{purine nucleobase transmembrane transport} were enriched in the DG, FA, SM and SQDG sets in both ontologies, ten enrichments in total, yet all derive from one purine-permease array on chromosome~6 (\texttt{SORBI\_3006G195000}, \texttt{SORBI\_3006G195100}, \texttt{SORBI\_3006G195200}; $r^2_{\max} \geq 0.99$). Collapsing on the gene set reduces them to the single locus they are. Second, a term can be broadly distributed and still uninformative. \textit{Zinc ion binding} was enriched in seven CTL sets and occupies six to eight independent intervals, but it is annotated to 1{,}015 of the 9{,}422 sorghum genes carrying any molecular-function term and is enriched only 1.7- to 4.1-fold. We therefore did not use interval count to select terms. It scales with how large a GO term is, and so discriminates against exactly the small, specific terms worth having: terms occupying three or more intervals average 175 background genes at 10-fold enrichment, while those below that threshold average 24 background genes at 23-fold. Selection instead used the specificity of the annotation, retaining loci whose term draws on at most 30 background genes.

Nineteen loci meet that criterion (Figure~\ref{fig:go_bp_main}), and they are distributed very unevenly between the trials. Seventeen come from LIN and two from CTL, and the two CTL terms, \textit{L-tyrosine catabolic process} and the molecular functions \textit{L-tyrosine-2-oxoglutarate transaminase} and \textit{nicotianamine aminotransferase activity}, share three of their four candidate genes and describe one tyrosine locus in the TG set rather than two findings. This asymmetry is not an artefact of the filter. CTL yielded 17 loci in total against 33 for LIN, from roughly half as many candidate genes (7{,}863 against 17{,}400 across both trait layers), and all but one of the CTL survivors were large, general categories such as \textit{proteolysis} (272 background genes), \textit{peptidase activity} (227) and \textit{zinc ion binding} (1{,}015). Under CTL the candidate sets are smaller and their annotation is unspecific; under LIN the enrichments name processes.

Within LIN, the sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG) set carries the largest and most coherent block, five class-specific loci plus two of the gene sets shared with other classes. Two of them speak directly to the nutrient regime: \textit{cellular response to phosphate starvation} (17.4-fold) and \textit{nitrate assimilation and transmembrane transport} (31.0-fold, shared with the FA set). Sulfolipid substitution for phospholipid is the canonical plant response to phosphate limitation, so recovering a phosphate-starvation signal among the genes associated with SQDG traits is consistent with the biochemistry rather than with the annotation alone. The remaining SQDG loci, \textit{ketoreductase activity} (32.3-fold), \textit{poly(A)-specific ribonuclease activity} (13.5-fold), \textit{negative regulation of response to salt stress} (11.1-fold) and \textit{pentosyltransferase activity} (10.3-fold), are enzymatic or regulatory activities without an established link to sulfolipid metabolism and are reported as annotated loci. Elsewhere in LIN, \textit{sphingolipid metabolic process} (32.5-fold) and \textit{regulation of defense response} (34.5-fold) fall in the AEG set, \textit{lipid transporter activity} (14.5-fold) and \textit{regulation of salicylic acid biosynthetic process} (13.8-fold) in TG, and \textit{steroid Delta-isomerase activity} (13.6-fold), \textit{phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase activity} (9.3-fold), \textit{carbon utilization} and \textit{anaphase-promoting complex-dependent catabolic process} (both 9.4-fold) in PC. Sphingolipid metabolism in the AEG set and lipid transport in the TG set are the two enrichments that name lipid biology directly.

CTL and LIN analyses produced distinct functional enrichments, but differences between enriched term lists are only qualitative. We therefore counted how many candidate genes the two conditions actually share (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig7_ctl_lin_overlap}), and asked whether that number exceeds what would be expected if the two sets had been drawn independently from the 34{,}027 annotated \textit{Sorghum bicolor} v3.1 gene models. Overlap was tested with a hypergeometric test and summarized as fold enrichment over chance and as the Jaccard index, the proportion of all candidate genes appearing in both conditions.

At the gene level the two sets overlapped strongly, with 354 genes shared against 148 expected ($2.39\times$), and the excess held in both trait layers (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}; Supplementary Tables~S21 to~S24). Jaccard indices were nonetheless low (0.020--0.069), so the excess involves few candidates. This mainly reflects LD, because each association tags every gene model in its window and gene-dense regions are tagged repeatedly in both trials. Collapsing to 250~kb loci reduces the CTL individual-lipid set from 1,100 genes to 378 loci and the LIN set from 4,323 genes to 971. Because no window size is objectively correct for collapsing linkage, we repeated the test at three resolutions and read the trend across them as the result. Individual-lipid enrichment decayed monotonically to chance by 250~kb, so loci recovered in the two trials are no more concordant than two independent samples of the same genome. The class-sum/ratio layer decayed more slowly and retained modest enrichment through 250~kb, making composite class-level traits the more reproducible unit across trials.
The per-class breakdown gives the same picture (Table~\ref{tab:overlap_class}). Only TG and PC were significantly enriched in the individual-lipid layer and both were weak, free fatty acids showed none, and SQDG, DG, AEG and terpenoids shared no candidate genes at all despite SQDG contributing 396 LIN candidates. Sharing a gene also rarely meant sharing a phenotype, since only 90 of the 354 genes recovered in both conditions (25.4\%) were associated with a lipid class in common, falling to 18.0\% in the individual-lipid layer.

What recurrence there is comes from very small regions. The 18 genes shared in the class-sum/ratio layer fall into two contiguous LD blocks, a ten-gene block on chromosome~10 (\texttt{SORBI\_3010G150325}--\texttt{SORBI\_3010G151325}) that contains the CMP-sialic acid transporter \texttt{SORBI\_3010G151000} discussed below, and an eight-gene block on chromosome~1 (\texttt{SORBI\_3001G168800}--\texttt{SORBI\_3001G171600}). The CTL and LIN lipid GWAS therefore recover largely non-overlapping genetic architectures, with candidate counts inflated several-fold by LD and only a few loci, mainly for class-level composite traits, recurring across the two field trials.

\subsection*{LINEX-based reaction mapping, reaction-balance scoring, and GWAS support}

To contextualize lipid associations biochemically, we used LINEX2 \citep{Rose2023LINEX2}, which integrates curated lipid-reaction data with measured lipid abundances to identify biochemical connections among lipids and enzymes, querying RHEA-annotated reactions \citep{Morgat2016Rhea}. As no sorghum reference exists in LINEX2, we used rice. The transfer of reaction annotations from rice to sorghum is unvalidated, so the resulting network is treated as a source of testable hypotheses rather than a sorghum-specific reaction set. The resulting network (Fig.~\ref{fig:Fig4_linex_rewiring_abc}A) revealed four candidate branches linking DG/MG, TG, and lysophospholipid pools, (i) PC+DG$\leftrightarrow$LPC+TG (LCAT-like transfer), (ii) TG$\leftrightarrow$DG (PNPLA1-labelled lipase branch), (iii) PE+DG$\leftrightarrow$TG+LPE (LRO1/PDAT-like transfer), and (iv) DG+MG$\leftrightarrow$TG (PNPLA3-labelled branch). These annotation-based hypotheses do not establish \emph{in vivo} flux direction, and because CTL and LIN were sampled in different years, all LIN--CTL contrasts are genotype-matched between-trial ratios rather than causal effects of management.

We converted each branch to a branch-aligned reaction-balance score per sample (Methods). The score is a within-sample log-ratio, the mean log abundance of a branch's products minus that of its substrates, and is therefore scale-invariant: a uniform multiplicative difference between the trials cancels exactly. All four branches shifted strongly and consistently in the expected direction, positive for PNPLA3 ($+0.327$), LCAT* ($+0.238$) and LRO1 ($+0.232$), and negative for PNPLA1 ($-0.268$), with paired genotype-matched Wilcoxon BH-adjusted $p<10^{-56}$ across 355 genotype pairs. Branches producing TG and their lysophospholipid coproducts shifted in the same direction, whereas the hydrolytic TG$\rightarrow$DG branch shifted oppositely, recapitulating the lipidome overview (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2_class_composition}) at the level of a small set of candidate reactions rather than independent class-level abundance shifts. We report these scores in Supplementary Table~S12 rather than in the main figure, with the per-trial medians given separately alongside their difference. Scale invariance removes the most obvious trial-level artefact but not one acting differentially on lipid classes, since a branch's products and substrates are different classes, and the trials remain confounded by year, planting date and acquisition batch; the direction of these shifts should therefore be read as a description of the two trials rather than as a response to management.

\subsection*{An interactive SoLD Shiny application enables exploration of lipid and GWAS results}

The SoLD web server provides an integrated environment for exploring lipid phenotypes and their genetic associations across two field environments (Fig.~\ref{fig:Fig5_shiny_app}A). Traits can be examined at three resolutions—individual species, class-level sums, and inter-class ratios \citep{Fahy2009LipidMapsClass}, with hierarchical filtering by class, subclass, and compound, as well as under alternative representations (Raw, \%TIC, CLR) to distinguish changes in absolute abundance from redistribution within the compositional lipidome. Exploratory and multivariate modules (data preview, boxplots, volcano plots, correlation and network views, heatmaps, PCA, t-SNE/UMAP) are paired with two genetic modules, (i) GWAS, which shows trait-specific associations, and (ii) Gene Hits, which aggregates recurrent candidate genes under user-defined dataset, trait-type, lipid-class, and significance settings. A Genotype module supports experimental design by identifying accessions within specified lipid value ranges.

\section{Discussion}

Lipidome differences between CTL and LIN were population-wide rather than driven by a few outlier genotypes, and the loci associated with lipid traits were almost entirely distinct between trials. We first relate lipidome-level changes to the conditions each trial imposed, then interpret CTL candidate genes, where variation reflects membrane organization and amino-acid metabolism, and LIN candidates, which converge on nutrient transport and status signaling. We then focus on LIN candidates on reactions flagged as remodeled by the network reconstruction and conclude with what the near-absence of shared loci implies about genetic control of the leaf lipidome across environments.

Two caveats apply. First, gene functions are assigned by orthology to characterized genes in other species, so they indicate what the gene family can do, not necessarily what the sorghum locus does in the field. Second, because candidate genes were drawn from LD blocks, most associated regions contain several genes, so an enrichment supported by fewer than three independent intervals likely reflects a single locus rather than a pathway.

\subsection*{Integrated interpretation of lipidome remodeling across contrasting field trials}

The main LIN lipidome change is a decline in SQDG accompanied by a rise in TG. The SQDG change is most informative because it opposes the canonical phosphate-starvation response, where phospholipids are replaced by non-phosphorus glycolipids and SQDG and DGDG typically increase \citep{Essigmann1998, Hartel2000, Jouhet2004, KellyDormann2002, Pfaff2020}. Reduced SQDG, therefore, does not indicate simple phosphate starvation and more likely reflects a mix of nutrient limitation, temperature, developmental state, and plastid-membrane turnover that this design cannot resolve. The candidate explains some of these changes. The LIN set included two SQD2-like sulfoquinovosyltransferase loci (\texttt{SORBI\_3003G076900}, \texttt{SORBI\_3002G000600}) annotated for sulfolipid biosynthesis. Both were identified via fatty-acid rather than SQDG phenotypes, so they are testable candidates for the sulfolipid component, not demonstrated effectors of SQDG abundance.

TG accumulation commonly results from low temperature, drought, oxidative stress, and nutrient-driven carbon reallocation \citep{Tan2018, Yan2025, Pfaff2020}. The LIN signature is consistent with increased storage, but the timing and carbon source for TG remain unclear. The accompanying change in DG–TG coordination is more specific. The ratios involving TG, DG, PE, and MGDG all shifted toward TG. Additionally, the PS–TG correlation switched sign between trials, and LINEX reaction balances were positive for the three TG-producing branches and negative for the hydrolytic TG-to-DG branch. These class-level balances, though not direct measures of enzyme activity, together indicate a reorganization of DG supply and TG synthesis and mobilization, rather than a uniform rise in neutral lipids.

A second change concerns phospholipid headgroup balance and lysophospholipid turnover. PS increased relative to other phospholipids, and its correlation with PC reversed, indicating headgroup redistribution within a reorganized phospholipid network rather than proportional expansion of the membrane pool. PE showed the largest gain in unsaturation among membrane classes, consistent with selective acyl-chain remodeling. Similar headgroup redistribution, membrane unsaturation, and lysophospholipid turnover occur under cold, nutrient, oxidative, and water limitations, where they are linked to membrane fluidity, lipid repair, signaling, and remobilization \citep{Hou2016, Rawat2021, Yan2025, Zhang2020}. However, PS and lysophospholipids each contribute well under 0.1\% of TIC, versus tens of percent for MGDG, so their consistent directional shifts reflect redistribution within small pools rather than bulk membrane change, and compositional transforms exaggerate proportional shifts in low-abundance species. PS is further qualified as the only trait clearly structured by genetic cluster, and only under LIN. We therefore regard the PS and lysophospholipid signatures as directionally robust but modest changes.

The LIN lipidome is best understood as a coordinated redistribution among plastid, phospholipid, lysophospholipid, and neutral-storage pools, consistently supported by composition, ratios, correlations, ontology enrichment, reaction balances, and GWAS. However, the experimental design limits inference, and targeted biochemical validation is needed to test the effects of nutrients, temperature, and developmental timing of the candidate genes.

\subsection*{Under CTL, lipid variation tracks membrane organization and amino-acid metabolism}

The CTL candidate sets link lipid composition to cellular processes beyond biosynthesis. The genes listed below were selected for recurring across phenotypes, belonging to an enriched category, or having a direct lipid-related annotation.

The clearest link to membrane lipid biology is \texttt{SORBI\_3006G040500}, a phospholipid-transporting ATPase associated with 27 TG phenotypes. It does not contribute to any enriched ontology term and is discussed for its annotation rather than enrichment. P4-type ATPases are flippases that move lipids between membrane leaflets to maintain phospholipid asymmetry. Biochemical work on the Arabidopsis P4-ATPase ALA2 shows strong specificity for PS, with PS head-group modification sharply reducing substrate recognition \citep{Theorin2019ALA2}. If the sorghum locus has a similar role, variation could alter membrane phospholipid organization or trafficking, shifting lipid intermediates between membrane and storage pools. This association does not show that it directly affects TG synthesis or that its substrate preference matches ALA2.

Enrichment of the \textit{L-tyrosine catabolic process} links lipid variation to primary metabolism and involves four genes on three chromosomes. Three encode class I/II aminotransferases, and the fourth, \texttt{SORBI\_3002G104200}, encodes 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase, which acts immediately downstream of tyrosine aminotransferase. Identifying two consecutive pathway steps from unlinked loci is the strongest aspect of this result. The aminotransferases are less straightforward. Two adjacent genes on chromosome~2 are assigned to the nicotianamine aminotransferase subfamily, which in grasses supplies mugineic-acid phytosiderophores for Fe uptake. In rice, OsNAAT1 has NAAT activity, is induced by Fe deficiency, and is required for phytosiderophore-mediated Fe uptake, with close homologs in maize and sorghum \citep{Inoue2008NAAT}. The third gene, \texttt{SORBI\_3005G152200} on chromosome~5, is assigned to the tyrosine aminotransferase subfamily and is not ambiguous. Thus the ambiguity lies in annotation of one locus, not in our interpretation, as reflected by molecular-function enrichment for both \textit{L-tyrosine-2-oxoglutarate transaminase activity} and \textit{nicotianamine aminotransferase activity} for the same three genes. Broader \textit{transaminase activity} and \textit{pyridoxal phosphate binding} enrichments add further unlinked loci, indicating aminotransferase involvement beyond the chromosome-2 region carrying the tyrosine-specific term.

Two observations support the degradative tyrosine pathway. First, every phenotype at the lead locus affected triacylglycerol species. Second, although tyrosine aminotransferase channels 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate into homogentisate-derived prenyllipid synthesis, $\alpha$-tocopherol was measured in SoLD and did not map to this locus, arguing against a tocopherol-biosynthetic cause. Homogentisate is also the entry to the degradative route ending in fumarate and acetoacetate, so flux through these steps feeds acetyl-CoA rather than prenyllipid head groups \citep{Schenck2018Tyrosine}. Arabidopsis data agree: \textit{tat1} mutants accumulate tyrosine, lose tocopherols, show reduced flux from tyrosine to fumarate and other TCA intermediates, and are more sensitive to darkness and sucrose depletion, identifying TAT1 as the main tyrosine degradation route \textit{in planta} under carbohydrate limitation \citep{Wang2019TAT}. Thus, a link via amino acid catabolism and carbon partitioning into storage lipid is the most parsimonious explanation, although functional characterization of the sorghum proteins is needed to confirm this.

The remaining CTL enrichments are weaker. \textit{Positive regulation of cytoplasmic translation} is not interpreted, because it failed the LD-aware null in the biological-process analysis and its molecular-function counterparts failed outright, with genes dominated by repeat-associated domains in gene-dense regions of chromosome~10. \textit{DNA replication initiation}, enriched among class-level candidates, includes the replication licensing factor MCM2, whose loss is embryonic-lethal in Arabidopsis and whose overexpression disrupts the root meristem \citep{Ni2009MCM2}. This likely reflects a link between proliferative state and lipid-class composition, since changes in cell division alter membrane demand and shift several classes together, rather than a direct link to lipid metabolism. Finally, the PA-associated oxidative-stress terms all reduce to a single class III peroxidase cluster, described six ways across the two ontologies. Suppression of the related Arabidopsis peroxidase AtPrx52 alters lignin content and redirects phenylpropanoid metabolism \citep{FernandezPerez2015Prx52}, showing that one plant peroxidase can affect both oxidative chemistry and carbon allocation. Thus the result indicates that variation in PA abundance coincides with peroxide-related processes at that locus, not a PA–peroxidase signalling mechanism.

Together, the CTL associations highlight membrane organization and amino-acid metabolism as the main interpretable signals, with growth state and redox processes as weaker, indirect contributors. Natural lipid variation in the panel seems to result from combined effects of membrane biology, metabolic state and growth, rather than a single controlling pathway.

\subsection*{Under LIN, candidates implicate nitrate transport, phosphate signaling and plastid sulfur supply}

The main theme among LIN candidates is nutrient transport and status signaling, spanning nitrate transport, phosphate signaling, and plastid sulfate supply relevant to SQDG metabolism. Nitrogen is represented by three transporters from two families. \texttt{SORBI\_3004G009400}, an NRT2.1-related high-affinity nitrate transporter, carries the nitrate assimilation and transport enrichments in both FA and SQDG candidate sets and associates with fatty-acid traits and FA/SQDG ratios; in maize, NRT2.1 requires NAR2.1 for high-affinity nitrate influx, and uptake capacity varies along the root axis with plasma-membrane H$^+$-ATPase activity \citep{Lupini2016NAR2, Sorgona2011Nitrate}. \texttt{SORBI\_3008G081400}, an NPF/NRT1-family transporter, shows the strongest LIN individual-lipid association and appears in transport terms under both conditions, while \texttt{SORBI\_3001G133900}, whose rice orthologue OsNPF2.4 is a low-affinity nitrate transporter mediating root-to-shoot transfer \citep{Xia2015NPF24}, carries the same annotation more weakly.

Nitrogen also appears in a storage rather than an uptake role. The chromosome 1 association at 1.0–1.2~Mb overlaps the dhurrin gene cluster, linking seven candidates to FA(22:1), 11,14,17-eicosatrienoic acid, TG(14:0\_18:3\_18:3) and twelve of thirteen PA-to-class ratios. Dhurrin is the cyanogenic glucoside that sorghum makes from L-tyrosine, and genes for its synthesis, vacuolar transport, and cyanide-free recycling co-occur in this cluster \citep{Wang2024Dhurrin}. Four candidates lie within it, annotated as a UDP-glucosyltransferase, a GSTL-type glutathione transferase, a MATE-family transporter, and a CYP71-family monooxygenase carrying the CYP71E1 reaction, matching the cluster’s known biosynthetic, transport, and recycling roles. Its relevance to LIN is that nitrogen transcriptionally induces dhurrin synthesis in older sorghum \citep{Busk2002Dhurrin}, and under reduced nitrogen, less tyrosine-derived carbon and nitrogen are allocated to dhurrin, with allelic variation in the cluster modulating this allocation. The presence of relative-abundance ratios rather than individual species is consistent with an effect on the precursor pool for lipid synthesis. Dhurrin was not measured in SoLD, so this interpretation relies solely on annotation within a single LD block.

Phosphorus and sulfur also enter via two SQDG-associated loci. \texttt{SORBI\_3001G028500} encodes an SPX-domain protein of the phosphate-status signaling family; in rice, OsSPX4 binds the central regulator PHR2 under phosphate sufficiency and is degraded under deficiency to activate the phosphate-starvation response \citep{Lv2014SPX4}. The sorghum locus showed phosphate-starvation enrichment and associated with the FA/SQDG and SQDG/FA ratios. \texttt{SORBI\_3006G244000} encodes a SULTR3-family sulfate transporter; in Arabidopsis, this subfamily resides in the chloroplast envelope and mediates over half of chloroplast sulfate uptake, with the quintuple mutant losing most sulfate import as well as cysteine and ABA \citep{Chen2019SULTR3}. The SQDG pattern makes this locus more informative. SQDG is a plastid lipid whose head group is built from sulfur imported into and reduced within the plastid, placing SULTR3 on the supply route for SQDG sulfur. Phosphate limitation alone should increase SQDG via replacement of phospholipids with non-phosphorous lipids, yet SQDG was depleted under LIN, which makes restricted plastidic sulfate supply a plausible hypothesis for follow-up rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Both loci remain correlative and untested in sorghum.

The purine-permease candidate \texttt{SORBI\_3006G195000} directly matches enriched purine-transport categories, and in rice the PUP11 permease influences seed setting and development via cytokinin distribution and starch biosynthesis \citep{Rong2024PUP11}. These categories are not independent evidence for a transport program, because they and the nitrate terms each reduce to a single tandem array, one locus recovered twenty-five times across the two ontologies. The broader signal is still distributed, since transmembrane transporter activity was enriched among LIN FA and SQDG candidates across many unlinked intervals, encompassing the nitrate, purine, and sulfate loci. Thus, transport function under LIN is supported at the general category level even though each substrate-specific term collapses to one array.

Two further transport candidates were recovered based on recurrence and annotation alone and did not contribute significantly to either ontology. \texttt{SORBI\_3003G169600}, a mitochondrial NAD$^+$ carrier related to Arabidopsis NDT1 \citep{Chaves2019NDT1}, was associated with AEG, DG, PC and TG lipid phenotypes, a range more consistent with an indirect effect via mitochondrial redox and energy balance than with any single lipid class. \texttt{SORBI\_3010G151000}, a CMP-sialic acid or nucleotide-sugar transporter \citep{Bakker2008CMPSia}, showed the broadest recurrence of any transport candidate, but because this rests on heterologous assays and a complete plant sialylation pathway remains uncertain, we interpret it as evidence for altered intracellular metabolite trafficking rather than a specific sialic-acid route.

\subsection*{Reaction-supported lipid-remodeling candidates under LIN implicate DG--TG cycling and phospholipid turnover}

Beyond the broader stress- and development-associated loci, the LINEX reaction network highlighted a focused set of lipid-remodelling candidates on branches connecting the DG, TG and lysophospholipid pools. Because LINEX assignments are annotation-guided and rice-referenced, these genes generate hypotheses rather than demonstrate enzymatic flux. The candidates below are ordered by proposed chemistry, from phospholipid input to triacylglycerol.

\texttt{SORBI\_3003G107900}, a non-specific phospholipase~C6, is not assigned to a single reaction but supplies substrate to others, as NPC enzymes convert phosphatidylcholine to diacylglycerol and phosphocholine, feeding the DG node from the phospholipid pool. Under LIN, the sorghum locus was associated with \mbox{PC(18:2\_20:4)}, a substrate of this reaction. In Arabidopsis, NPC2 and NPC6 redundantly support triacylglycerol production during pollen tube growth; their overexpression induces lipid-droplet accumulation \citep{Bose2021NPC}, and knock-down lines have reduced root growth rescued by exogenous phosphocholine \citep{Ngo2019NPC}. That work also defines the nutrient context: nitrogen limitation promotes triacylglycerol accumulation at the expense of phospholipids, and NPC-mediated head-group hydrolysis is a plausible entry point. However, the characterized phenotypes are in roots and pollen rather than leaves, and the phosphate-responsive Arabidopsis isoforms are NPC4 and NPC5, not NPC6, so this locus is unlikely to participate in the phosphate-status response described above.

Branch (i), the hypothesized PC$+$DG$\leftrightarrow$LPC$+$TG transfer, has two candidates from the lecithin--cholesterol acyltransferase superfamily. \texttt{SORBI\_3006G214500} was linked under LIN to \mbox{DG(18:2\_18:3)} and \mbox{MG(18:2)}, the branch's diacylglycerol and monoacylglycerol substrates, while \texttt{SORBI\_3004G341900}, a phospholipid--sterol O-acyltransferase, uses phosphatidylcholine and releases lysophosphatidylcholine, the branch product. Both assignments remain uncertain, since in Arabidopsis a related LCAT-family protein functions not as a lecithin:cholesterol or PDAT-like transferase but as a cytosolic, calcium-independent phospholipase~A specific for phosphatidylcholine at sn-2 \citep{Chen2012}. Thus the association supports phospholipid deacylation and LPC production on this branch without demonstrating that either locus catalyzes the depicted transfer.

\textit{DGAT1} is the clearest lipid-genetic link to the TG-enrichment phenotype and the strongest candidate. Five of six phenotypes associated with \texttt{SORBI\_3010G170000} were triacylglycerol species with polyunsaturated acyl chains, including \mbox{TG(18:3\_18:3\_18:3)} and \mbox{TG(18:2\_18:2\_18:4)}. Sorghum mapping has repeatedly identified a chromosome~10 QTL for crude fat and gross energy whose interval contains this gene, the sorghum homolog of maize \textit{DGAT1}, with the TX15 peak falling within its transcript. Because DGAT catalyzes the final step of triacylglycerol biosynthesis and these TGs are among the most unsaturated in the panel, this supports increased channelling of DG into TG under LIN \citep{Boyles2017DGAT1, Yen2008DGAT}. Together with the phospholipase~C candidate above, this places two consecutive steps of one route, PC to DG and DG to TG, among the LIN candidates.

The reverse reaction is also shown. \texttt{SORBI\_3001G041900} was associated with three triacylglycerol species and, at class level, with total TG and the TG/SQDG and SQDG/TG ratios, consistent with a lipase acting on the bulk TG pool rather than on individual species. In Arabidopsis, the patatin lipases \textit{SDP1} and \textit{SDP1L} preferentially hydrolyse TG over DG or MG and mediate most TG breakdown during seed reserve mobilization \citep{Kelly2011SDP1L}. A sorghum survey placed this locus in the same clade as \textit{SbSDP1-L} \citep{Sapara2024PLA}, making it a strong candidate for TG--DG turnover under LIN.

A second module lies outside the DG–TG branches. Among lipid-pathway terms, \textit{sphingolipid metabolic process} was the clearest ontology signal combining specific pathway annotation with support from three independent intervals, and its three genes span three consecutive steps on three chromosomes. \texttt{SORBI\_3001G434900} encodes serine C-palmitoyltransferase, the committed first step in sphingoid-base synthesis. \texttt{SORBI\_3007G041500} is a DAGK-domain kinase linked to sphingolipid metabolism. \texttt{SORBI\_3003G394100} encodes a ceramide glucosyltransferase that diverts ceramide into glucosylceramide. All three associate with the same trait, AEG(o-15:2\_15:0), which is the third largest compositional shift between the trials and the only ether-linked species to increase under LIN. The trait carrying the module is therefore one of the clearest between-trial differences in the dataset, although the module itself rests on the genetic evidence rather than on lipid abundance, since the sphingolipid category as a whole barely moves and its measured species are trace components. This is nonetheless the most complete candidate pathway recovered in the study and the most direct target for functional work, with the caveat that three loci associated with one trait are equally consistent with independent effects on a shared phenotype as with a single regulatory module.

Together these loci implicate phospholipid head-group hydrolysis, DG-pool input, and both arms of DG–TG cycling, though they were detected independently rather than as a single module and each step still requires functional testing in sorghum. Additional LIN candidates share these reaction-family annotations, including two patatin-domain lipases (\texttt{SORBI\_3003G389400}, \texttt{SORBI\_3002G031800}) and a phospholipid/glycerol acyltransferase (\texttt{SORBI\_3001G026100}), but these mainly associate with fatty-acid and PC traits rather than the network’s triacylglycerol species, indicating variation within the enzyme families without pinpointing specific reactions.

\subsection*{The genetic architecture of the sorghum leaf lipidome is strongly environment-dependent}

The candidate loci above came from trials differing in nutrient supply, planting date, management and year, and their functional enrichments diverged accordingly. Direct comparison makes this divergence clear. After collapsing linkage blocks, the individual-lipid loci recovered under CTL and LIN overlap no more than expected by chance for two independent genome samples ($1.04\times$ at 250~kb), and among genes recovered in both trials only a quarter were linked to the same lipid class, so even this limited overlap is largely not trait-specific. The much stronger apparent gene-level excess before collapsing reflects mapping rather than biology, with linkage structure inflating candidate counts three- to five-fold. Such cross-study comparisons should therefore be made at the locus, not the gene, level.

Two positive findings emerge. First, class-level composite traits replicated better than individual species, retaining significant cross-trial excess at 100 and 250~kb where individual lipids did not. Aggregating species into class totals reduces measurement noise and averages over acyl-chain heterogeneity, making composites the more suitable unit for cross-environment comparison and the level at which lipidome-wide remodelling signatures were defined. Second, the recurrent loci cluster into two chromosomal regions, one containing the CMP-sialic acid transporter \texttt{SORBI\_3010G151000}, itself among the most recurrent LIN candidates. Loci detected in both trials and across many class-level traits are the strongest starting points for validation.

This pattern indicates that genetic control of leaf lipid composition in the SAP is strongly environment-dependent, matching the contrasting genomic heritabilities in the two trials. It does not mean individual associations are false, since allelic effects can be real and reproducible within a given environment yet fail to generalize beyond it. Because CTL and LIN were sampled in different years and field contexts, genotype-by-nutrient effects cannot be distinguished from genotype-by-year or genotype-by-management effects. Resolving this requires growing the same contrast in a common year under factorial nutrient treatments, so interaction terms can be estimated directly rather than inferred from non-overlapping candidate sets. Until then, the loci recurring in both trials, and the trait classes where they recur, are the most efficient targets for such an experiment.

\section*{Conclusion}

SoLD establishes a population-scale lipidomics resource for the sorghum association panel and shows that two contrasting field contexts drive extensive, population-wide lipidome remodelling. Across 243 annotated species, LIN differed from CTL by four signatures: SQDG depletion, TG enrichment, PS-centered phospholipid redistribution and a shifted LPC/LPE balance. These patterns were consistently detected across abundance contrasts, compositional analyses, chemical-space summaries and lipid ontology enrichment, and were independent of botanical race and genome-wide genetic structure, indicating coordinated redistribution among interconnected lipid pools rather than isolated class shifts or artefacts of population stratification.

Genetic analysis nominates candidates for the two strongest signatures. For TG enrichment, the main candidate is \texttt{SORBI\_3010G170000} (\textit{DGAT1}), which catalyzes the committed step of triacylglycerol synthesis, associates almost exclusively with polyunsaturated TG species, and lies within a known sorghum crude-fat QTL. The same route includes \texttt{SORBI\_3003G107900}, a non-specific phospholipase~C6 that converts phosphatidylcholine to diacylglycerol and whose Arabidopsis orthologues are required for TG production, and \texttt{SORBI\_3001G041900}, an SDP1-like lipase that performs the reverse reaction and associates with total TG. These jointly implicate phospholipid head-group hydrolysis, TG synthesis and TG turnover among the LIN candidates, a plausible route for TG accumulation at the expense of phospholipids under the combined LIN field context. For SQDG depletion, \texttt{SORBI\_3006G244000}, a SULTR3-family sulfate transporter supplying most chloroplast sulfate, lies on the route to the sulfoquinovose head group and predicts the observed change, whereas the canonical phosphate-starvation response does not. The phosphate-signalling locus \texttt{SORBI\_3001G028500} and two SQD2-like sulfoquinovosyltransferases also appear among these candidates. A third group affects membrane rather than storage lipids, including a sphingolipid-biosynthetic module spanning three unlinked loci on chromosomes 1, 3 and 7, and a TIFY/JAZ cluster associated specifically with $n$-3 polyunsaturated lipids, the substrate pool for jasmonate synthesis.

These are candidates, not causes. All rely on linkage-based mapping and cross-species annotation, and enrichment analysis showed that once linkage is considered, most functional signals in either condition collapse to single genomic intervals. The candidate sets themselves barely overlap between trials, sharply limiting how far any locus can generalize. Class-level composite traits were the exception, showing modest cross-trial recurrence and making them the more reproducible unit for cross-environment work.

Three lines of work follow. Functional validation should start with loci that combine strong association, a mechanistically matched phenotype, and a testable direction—\textit{DGAT1}, the SULTR3 transporter, and the phospholipase~C locus—using knockouts or allelic contrasts scored for the lipid classes they are predicted to affect. Distinguishing genotype-by-nutrient from genotype-by-year effects requires growing both regimes in a single year under factorial nutrient treatments, so interaction terms can be estimated directly. Finally, the resource, including the interactive interface for exploring lipid traits and associations, is designed to let others test hypotheses about sorghum lipid variation that this panel and these two environments cannot resolve alone.

\section*{Materials and methods}

\subsection*{Plant Material and Growth Conditions}

We used the Sorghum Association Panel ($\sim$400 genotypes), a genetically diverse collection of sorghum accessions representing major botanical races and broad phenotypic diversity \citep{Casa2008CommunityResourcesSorghum, Boatwright2022}. We evaluated SAP accessions across two different field settings during two growing seasons (2019 and 2022) at the Pee Dee Research and Education Center, Clemson University, Florence, South Carolina. The ``control'' condition, herein denoted as CTL, involved standard agronomic inputs with sufficient levels of nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) along with a typical planting schedule. In contrast, the ``low input'' condition, denoted as LIN, featured reduced N and P coupled with earlier planting to mimic a cold stress environment.

\subsection*{Schematic diagram}

The schematic in Supplementary~\ref{fig:suppfig_s1_workflow} was created using FigureLabs.ai (\url{https://www.figurelabs.ai/}).

\subsection*{Lipidomics Analysis}

\paragraph{Sample Preparation and Extraction.}
Samples were prepared following the standard extraction protocols explained in the paper by \citep{Barnes2022}.

\paragraph{Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) Analysis.}
Lipid extracts were analyzed at the Joint Genome Institute/Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory using their standard LC-MS/MS electrospray ionization lipidomics workflow; detailed chromatographic conditions, acquisition parameters, and quality-control procedures are described in the published JGI/LBNL protocol using a Thermo Exploris 120 Orbitrap mass spectrometer inline with an Agilent 1290 UHPLC system \citep{Louie2025JGI_Lipids}.

\paragraph{Feature Detection and Data Processing.}
Raw LC-MS data, in both positive and negative ion modes, were processed utilizing MZmine 2, an open-source software for the analysis of mass spectrometry data \citep{Pluskal2010MZmine2}. The pipeline encompassed peak detection, chromatogram construction, deconvolution, and isotope filtering, producing a detailed feature table containing mass-to-charge ratio-retention time (mz-rt) pairs. Isotopic peaks were excluded to minimize redundancy, as a single metabolite can yield multiple co-eluting ions, such as adducts and in-source fragments. Therefore, mz-rt duplicates were handled with care, with potential de-adducting considered via MS-FLO when appropriate. We acknowledge that such degeneracy can lead to an inflated number of features compared to the actual number of metabolites present, which we considered during metabolite identification.

\paragraph{Blank/extraction-control filtering, intensity thresholds, and sparsity pruning.}

To reduce background and carryover effects, an extraction control filter was implemented at the feature level. For each feature, the maximum intensity was determined across extraction controls ($a$) and biological samples ($b$). Features for which $b < 10\times a$ were eliminated. To preclude the exclusion of borderline yet potentially biological signals, a feature was retained if at least one biological sample exceeded the extraction control maximum. Furthermore, a minimum average intensity threshold within the treatment groups of interest ($\sim 10^{6}$ peak height) was imposed to ensure that downstream analyses would emphasize robust signals. At the sample level, any sample exhibiting $\geq 70\%$ features as zero (or missing) was excluded prior to normalization and statistical analysis. This pruning of sparsity is essential to prevent unstable scaling and spurious differential signals caused by ultra-sparse profiles. A lipid species was considered detected within a condition if it passed the feature-detection, intensity, and sparsity filters described above using only that condition's samples. Species detected in both conditions were classified as shared, whereas species detected in only one condition were classified as condition-specific (CTL-specific or LIN-specific).

\paragraph{MS/MS spectral library matching and cross-referencing of IDs.}
For each feature analyzed by MS/MS, the most intense fragmentation spectrum was queried against the GNPS database. Library matches resulted in putative identifications (Metabolomics Standards Initiative levels 2/3), potentially including isomers or near-mass analogs. Species-level assignments are therefore putative throughout, and individual annotations should be confirmed against authentic standards before being treated as identified compounds. Features without direct matches were eliminated. To enable quantification with identifications, tables were linked using the feature \emph{row ID} from the MZmine peak list and the corresponding \#Scan\# key in the GNPS results, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between intensities and candidate identifications. In cases where a feature yielded multiple GNPS hits, a single primary annotation was designated by retaining the highest MQScore (cosine similarity). Ties in the values were resolved based on a greater number of shared fragment ions and a smaller precursor mass error (ppm). All other sub-threshold or lower-ranked candidates were retained for verification but were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses.

\paragraph{Systematic Error Removal Using Random Forest (SERRF).}
Following the cleaning process, the data were then used for SERRF normalization \citep{Fan2019}. We used the SERRF server (\url{https://slfan2013.github.io/SERRF-online/}) to obtain the normalized output. After applying SERRF, only biological samples were preserved. Any zeros were substituted with two-thirds of the minimum nonzero value for that feature to prevent potential infinite logarithmic transformations.

\paragraph{Spatial Correction.}
Finally, we conducted an additional quality control step specifically aimed at eliminating any spatial patterns across our experimental trials. This was achieved using the R package \texttt{SpATS} \citep{RodriguezAlvarez2018}, which applies a two-dimensional P-spline ANOVA surface over the field coordinates. For every lipid feature, we characterized its intensity as
\begin{align}
  y_{ij} &= \mu + f_{\mathrm{row}}(i) + f_{\mathrm{col}}(j) + f_{\mathrm{row,col}}(i,j) + \varepsilon_{ij},
\end{align}
where
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f_{\mathrm{row}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{col}}$ represent smooth functions that model systematic effects across rows and columns, respectively,
  \item $f_{\mathrm{row,col}}$ is a smooth interaction surface that handles more complex spatial gradients.
\end{itemize}
For downstream analyses we used SpATS-adjusted intensities, calculated as the model residuals plus the fitted mean. This preserves the original intensity scale and avoids negative values, which is required for the \%TIC, CLR, and log-ratio transforms. This methodology effectively corrects for positional artifacts, such as edge effects, that could interfere with subsequent analyses. Detailed smoothing parameters, including the number of knots, penalty orders, and comprehensive model specifications, can be found in our GitHub repository.

\paragraph{Lipid Quantification.}
The identified lipid species were organized into lipid classes and subclasses based on Lipid Maps \citep{Fahy2009LipidMapsClass} (Supplementary Table~S15). In each sample, the total intensity for a class was obtained by summing the intensities of the species within that class. To manage variability in signal intensity due to different runs or injections, these class totals were normalized relative to the total ion current (TIC) of the sample. As a result, the relative abundances were presented as percentages of the TIC by adding up intensities across all lipid classes in the sample. For each class and its subclasses, we determined the TIC fraction for each sample and then averaged these percentages across samples for each condition. Lipids were categorized into glycerolipid, glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, sterol, betaine lipid, fatty acid, ether lipid, and terpenoid.

\paragraph{Lipid ratio calculation.}
For each sample $i$ and lipid class $c$, species intensities were aggregated to class totals,
\[
C_{i,c}=\sum_{k \in c} I_{i,k}.
\]
Two annotated features were excluded from all class- and category-level comparisons. Phytosphingosine rose from 0.04\% of total signal under CTL to 16.9\% under LIN, which would make a free sphingoid long-chain base the second most abundant feature of the LIN lipidome and is not biologically plausible for a leaf. SM(d18:1/17:0) was the only member of its class, and plants do not synthesize sphingomyelin, so an odd-chain C17 species of this type is most consistent with an internal standard or misannotation. Both differences track the separate 2019 and 2022 analytical runs rather than genotype. Neither feature yielded a GWAS candidate, so no genetic result depends on this exclusion. Because log-ratios are scale-invariant, TIC normalization is not required for ratio computation. To handle zero class totals, we used a sample-specific pseudo-count,
\[
\varepsilon_i=0.5 \cdot \min\{C_{i,c} : C_{i,c}>0\},
\]
and computed
\[
\ell_{i,c}=\log_{10}(C_{i,c}+\varepsilon_i).
\]
Pairwise class log-ratios were then
\[
r_{i,c_1/c_2}=\ell_{i,c_1}-\ell_{i,c_2}.
\]

\paragraph{Compositional transforms.}
Class totals and the pairwise class log-ratios defined above are the traits carried forward to GWAS. For species-resolved analyses we used the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation, which rescales each species by the geometric mean of all species in the same sample and so removes the constant-sum constraint of compositional data:
\[
\mathrm{CLR}(x_i)=\ln\!\left(\frac{x_i}{g(\mathbf{x})}\right),
\]
where $g(\mathbf{x})$ is the geometric mean of species-level abundances in a sample.

\subsection*{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on individual lipid species within each experimental condition. Lipid intensities were first normalized to total ion current (TIC) to obtain relative abundances, then transformed using a centered log-ratio (CLR). The CLR-transformed data were mean-centered and scaled to unit variance, and PCA was conducted in R using the \emph{stats} package. The first two principal components were retained for visualization.

\subsection*{Genome-wide Association Studies (GWAS)}

GWAS analyses were carried out for each lipid trait under each condition using the mixed linear model (MLM) featured in GEMMA (v2.3) \citep{Zhou2012}. To address population structure and relatedness, a centered relatedness matrix (kinship) was computed from SNP genotype data. We filtered the genotype data by MAF 0.05 and heterozygosity of 0.8. For each lipid trait, the MLM was applied using the kinship matrix to handle population stratification effects. Besides individual traits, GWAS was also applied to summed lipid classes and all possible ratios. The first three genotype principal components were fitted as fixed covariates. A Bonferroni-corrected genome-wide significance threshold was employed to account for multiple comparisons. Based on approximately 6.11 million tested SNPs under CTL and 6.08 million under LIN, this corresponded to $p \le 8.19\times10^{-9}$ for CTL ($-\log_{10}(p)\ge 8.09$) and $p \le 8.22\times10^{-9}$ for LIN ($-\log_{10}(p)\ge 8.09$).

\subsection*{Botanical race and population-structure testing}

Botanical race, working group, and marker-based genetic cluster assignments for the SAP were taken from panel passport data. For race analysis, accessions were grouped into the five classical races (Bicolor, Caudatum, Durra, Guinea, Kafir) plus a Mixed group containing all hyphenated intermediate designations; accessions lacking a race assignment and the single \textit{S. bicolor} subsp. \textit{verticilliflorum} accession were excluded. The second grouping used the $K=6$ genetic cluster assignment. Fourteen traits were tested per condition: total lipid signal (log$_{10}$ of the summed SpATS-adjusted intensity across all annotated lipid features) and the \%TIC of each of 13 major lipid classes. Groups with fewer than five accessions in a condition were excluded from that test. Differences among groups were assessed with tie-corrected Kruskal--Wallis tests within each condition, and $P$-values were adjusted across the 14 traits per condition using the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure. Effect sizes were computed as $\varepsilon^2=(H-k+1)/(n-k)$, where $H$ is the tie-corrected statistic, $k$ the number of groups, and $n$ the number of accessions; negative values were truncated to zero. Because total ion current is partly technical, the total-lipid trait is reported for completeness and interpreted only relatively.

The stability of the class composition within a trial was assessed by resampling rather than by hypothesis testing. Neither procedure poses a null hypothesis, computes a test statistic, or yields a $P$-value. The first is a leave-one-group-out sensitivity analysis: within a trial, every accession belonging to one botanical race group was deleted and the mean \%TIC of each of the 13 lipid classes was recomputed from the remaining accessions, once for each of the six groups, giving 78 recomputations per trial. We report the smallest and largest value any omission produced and the largest absolute departure from the full-panel mean. The second is nonparametric subsampling: 1000 draws of 80\% of a trial's accessions were taken without replacement (315 of 394 under CTL, 290 of 363 under LIN, random seed fixed at 1), the same 13 class means were recomputed for each draw, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distribution are reported as a percentile interval. That interval describes how far the descriptive mean moves under resampling; it is not a confidence interval derived from a test, and it carries no significance claim. Both procedures run entirely within a single trial and involve no comparison between trials.

\subsection*{Candidate-locus definition and cross-trial overlap testing}

Cross-trial overlap was computed from the LD-mapped candidate tables, separately for the individual-lipid layer, the class-sum/ratio layer, and their union. At gene level, the background was the 34{,}027 annotated \textit{S. bicolor} v3.1 gene models. Because LD-based mapping assigns every gene within a window to one association, gene-level counts overstate independent signals, so we repeated the analysis at locus level. The genome was partitioned into non-overlapping 100, 250, and 500~kb windows, and a window was a candidate locus for a condition if it contained at least one candidate gene from that condition. The locus-level background was the set of windows containing at least one annotated gene (4{,}844, 2{,}279, and 1{,}239 windows at 100, 250, and 500~kb, respectively). At both resolutions we assessed overlap with a one-sided hypergeometric (Fisher right-tail) test, and reported $P$-values with the fold enrichment over the expected overlap $|A||B|/N$ and the Jaccard index $|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$, which is insensitive to set-size disparities between conditions.

For the per-class analysis, each candidate gene was assigned the lipid classes represented among the phenotypes with which it was significantly associated. Class labels were parsed from the lipid species name for individual-lipid traits and from the numerator and denominator of the trait name for class sums and ratios; species lacking an abbreviated class prefix were mapped via the curated lipid class table. Because a single gene in the class-ratio layer can be associated with ratios spanning many classes, per-class overlap tests were stratified by trait layer. Class concordance was the proportion of genes recovered in both conditions that shared at least one lipid class between their CTL and LIN class sets. All tests were implemented in Python (pandas, NumPy), with hypergeometric, $\chi^2$, and Benjamini--Hochberg procedures implemented directly.

\subsection*{Gene Annotation}

SNPs were aligned with the \textit{Sorghum bicolor} reference genome v3.1 (BTx623). Linkage disequilibrium in the sorghum association panel extends over appreciable physical distances \citep{Boatwright2022}, so a Bonferroni-significant SNP typically tags several linked genes rather than one. Significant SNPs were therefore grouped into association regions at a linkage-disequilibrium threshold of $r^2 \geq 0.4$. This is a moderate cutoff, retaining variants that carry at least approximately 40\% of the effective sample size for tagging a causal site, and it trades the narrow regions obtained at stringent thresholds, which discard genes tagged by imperfect proxies, against the inflated gene counts obtained at permissive ones. Because candidate-gene counts are considerably more sensitive to this cutoff than interval counts are, all interval-level analyses, including GO enrichment and the cross-trial overlap, were carried out on 250~kb intervals rather than on gene identifiers. Candidate genes were defined as the annotated gene models falling within these LD-defined association regions. Functional annotations and homology were obtained from Phytozome (\url{https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov}), SorghumBase (\url{https://sorghumbase.com}), and TAIR for corresponding \textit{Arabidopsis thaliana} orthologs. Genes with known roles in N, P, cold tolerance, or lipid metabolism were specifically noted. We aggregated the frequency of each candidate gene within all lipid GWAS findings and marked those with the highest recurrence among Bonferroni-significant associations.

\subsubsection*{Gene Ontology Enrichment}
GO biological-process and molecular-function terms were taken from the same PANTHER-derived annotation. The two ontologies were analyzed separately, each against a background restricted to sorghum genes carrying at least one annotation in that ontology, and $p$-values were BH-corrected within each ontology and analysis. Over-representation was tested with a right-tailed hypergeometric test, retaining terms with ≥3 candidate genes and ≥5 background genes, and BH-correcting $p$-values across all GO terms per analysis. To account for LD among candidate genes, each term was also tested against a permutation null that preserved the candidate block structure. We used 8,000 draws, which bounds the smallest attainable permutation $p$-value at $1.25\times10^{-4}$, well below the resolution needed after BH correction. The genome was divided into consecutive, non-overlapping 250~kb intervals, assigning each candidate gene to the interval containing its start. Thus, the number of intervals carrying a term reflects the number of distinct genomic regions supporting that term, not the number of gene IDs. Each permutation redrew the same number of intervals, each contributing the same number of genes, from background intervals matched on gene density. Permutation $p$-values were BH-corrected and reported as $q_{\mathrm{perm}}$.

Enrichment was computed separately within each lipid class, so that a term is attributed to the traits of one class rather than to the candidate set as a whole. Crossed with the two trait layers, the two ontologies and the two trials, this gives one test per class per layer per ontology per trial.

Because the Gene Ontology nests parent and child terms, a single candidate gene set can surface as several terms. Terms whose candidate gene sets were identical were therefore merged into one finding, keeping the most specific term as the label and retaining every merged term name and GO identifier in the supplementary table. The same gene set also recurs between strata, since class sums and ratios share candidate genes by construction: one purine permease array on chromosome~6 appeared ten times, across four lipid classes, both trait layers and both ontologies, described once as a process and once as an activity. Gene sets identical across strata were merged in the same way, and the lipid classes in which each appeared were retained, because a locus surfacing in a single class is class-specific whereas one surfacing across many is generic. After both merges, the 101 terms passing the LD-aware null reduced to 50 distinct loci.

For the main figure we retained loci whose GO term draws on at most 30 background genes. This is a criterion of specificity rather than of significance, applied because a term drawn from a background of hundreds or thousands of genes cannot be interpreted whatever its enrichment: zinc ion binding (1{,}015 background genes), transmembrane transport (526), nucleic acid binding (522) and mRNA binding (432) all pass the permutation null, and none of them names a lipid-related process. Two further terms, \textit{detection of stimulus} and \textit{DNA topological change}, were removed by name as uninterpretable in this context. Nineteen loci remain and are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:go_bp_main}; all 50 are listed in Supplementary Table~S16 together with the number of independent 250~kb intervals supporting each, and the uncollapsed per-term results in Supplementary Tables~S17 and~S18. Interval count is reported but was not used to select or to rank terms. It scales with the size of the GO term, so a threshold on it discriminates against precisely the small, specific terms of interest: terms supported by three or more intervals averaged 175 background genes at 10-fold enrichment, while those below that threshold averaged 24 background genes at 23-fold.

\subsubsection*{Lipid Metabolic Network Analysis (LINEX2)}

We used the Lipid Network Explorer (LINEX2; \url{https://exbio.wzw.tum.de/linex/}) to reconstruct annotation-guided lipid reaction networks and identify plausible biochemical connections among lipid classes and candidate enzymes. Lipid names were standardized to align with species notation (class plus acyl composition). LINEX2 constructs a global species network based on curated reaction rules, including headgroup interconversions, (de)acylation/editing, elongation, and desaturation, and maps these relationships onto the observed lipid abundance data. Since \textit{Sorghum bicolor} is not implemented as a default organism in LINEX2, we selected \textit{Oryza sativa} (OSA) as the reference species for network construction.

For branch-level analysis, we extracted candidate reactions linking DG/MG, TG, and lysophospholipid pools and summarized each branch using a reaction-balance score. Three were taken from the LINEX2 network. The fourth, an LCAT-like acyl-editing branch denoted LCAT*, was defined manually from reaction specificity (RHEA:32843, RHEA:44236) rather than retrieved from LINEX2, and the asterisk marks it as such throughout. For sample $i$ and lipid class $c$, let $C_{i,c}$ denote the class-sum abundance of class $c$ in sample $i$, let $\varepsilon_i$ denote the sample-specific pseudocount added prior to log transformation, and define $\ell_{i,c}=\log_{10}(C_{i,c}+\varepsilon_i)$ as the corresponding log-abundance. For each candidate reaction, we computed
\[
S_{i,\mathrm{rxn}}=\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{products}})-\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{substrates}}),
\]
so that larger $S_{i,\mathrm{rxn}}$ indicates a more product-weighted balance within that sample, whereas smaller values indicate a more substrate-weighted balance. The branch-specific scores were defined as:
\begin{itemize}
\item LCAT*: $S_i=\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{LPC}},\ell_{i,\mathrm{TG}})-\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{PC}},\ell_{i,\mathrm{DG}})$;
\item PNPLA1: $S_i=\ell_{i,\mathrm{DG}}-\ell_{i,\mathrm{TG}}$;
\item LRO1: $S_i=\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{TG}},\ell_{i,\mathrm{LPE}})-\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{PE}},\ell_{i,\mathrm{DG}})$;
\item PNPLA3: $S_i=\ell_{i,\mathrm{TG}}-\mathrm{mean}(\ell_{i,\mathrm{DG}},\ell_{i,\mathrm{MG}})$.
\end{itemize}
These scores were treated as branch-aligned summaries of lipid-pool balance rather than direct measures of enzymatic activity or metabolic flux. Reaction-balance scores were compared between CTL and LIN using paired genotype-matched Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and $p$-values were adjusted using the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure.

\subsubsection*{Lipid Ontology Enrichment and Hierarchical Classification (LION/web)}

We conducted a functional ontology for lipids using LION/web \citep{Molenaar2019} (\url{http://www.lipidontology.com}). Lipid nomenclature was standardized according to LIPID MAPS annotations. The default settings of LION/web were applied for enrichment statistics and multiple-testing correction. We retained terms for $q \leq 0.10$. A hierarchical classification analysis of individual lipids and functional ontologies was also performed using LION/web.
